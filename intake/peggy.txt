My mum is Peggy. She's eighty-three and she lives on her own in the same
terraced house she and Dad bought in 1968, in Ashton, just outside Manchester.
Dad died six years ago. She's got no intention of moving and I've stopped
asking.

She went into the mill at fifteen — Hartley's, the clothing factory off the main
road. She was a machinist. She started on linings and ended up running the
sample room, which meant she was the one who made the first version of anything
new before it went to the floor. Forty-one years, same place, until it shut in
1998. She always says the factory didn't close, it "went to Turkey". She can
still look at a jacket and tell you whether it was made properly, and she's
usually rude about it.

She was properly good at it. She could set up an overlocker, she knew which
needle for which cloth, and she used to alter things for half the street in the
evenings — wedding dresses, taking up trousers, all of it, for nothing. She
sewed until her hands got too stiff, maybe five or six years ago, and I know she
misses it more than she says.

Things she actually cares about now: Manchester City, seriously, she's watched
them since the sixties and she has opinions about the money. And dancing — she
and Dad did ballroom at the Ritz, and she still has the big band stuff on, Joe
Loss, Glenn Miller, that sort of thing.

She loves the family — I've got two brothers, there are six grandkids and two
great-grandkids now, and she knows every birthday. She loves Ashton itself, or
the Ashton she remembers, and she'll tell you what used to be where the retail
park is.

Health: she's on tablets for blood pressure and thyroid, and something for her
heart, I think four or five altogether. Morning and evening. She's had a knee
replacement and she walks with a stick outside but not indoors. Her hearing's
gone a bit in one ear and she won't wear the aid. Her eyes are fine. Her mind is
completely fine — she's sharper than me and she does not like being talked to
like she's daft. That's the thing that would upset her most, honestly. If
someone's slow or patronising with her she just goes quiet and gets off the
phone.

She talks normally, quite fast actually, Manchester accent. She doesn't hesitate
or lose her thread.

I'm in Bristol so it's a four-hour drive and I've got the kids and work. I ring
her Sunday nights and sometimes Wednesday if I can. My brothers are better at
visiting than phoning. It's not that she's on her own with nobody — she's got
all of us — it's just that most days nobody actually speaks to her, and I do
worry about that. Her neighbour Sylvia pops in but she's eighty herself.
